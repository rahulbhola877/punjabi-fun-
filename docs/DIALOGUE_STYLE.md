# Street talk: a style guide

How people in Gedi Route speak, so new lines sound like the same street. Lines are in Roman Punjabi (Gurmukhi sounds, Latin letters), the way people text.

## Voice
- Short. One breath: 4 to 12 words. A bubble must be readable in two seconds.
- Warm teasing, not insults. Quarrels are about nothing (a buffalo, a cricket umpire, a card that never came), never about religion, caste, region or looks.
- Mix as people do: Punjabi first, English words where Punjab uses them (match, phone, recharge, license, petrol, helmet).
- Elders say *puttar*, *beta*, *oye*; the young say *paaji*, *yaar*, *bro*. Women of the gali say *bhenji*, *ni*, *hai rabba*.

## By closeness
| They feel | They say |
|---|---|
| cold | clipped, no name: "Hmph. Bol, ki chahida?" |
| know you | Sat Sri Akal, your name, the weather or the hour |
| friend | *mera sher*, offers chah, asks after one of the family by name |
| family | *ghar da bacha*, food, "dass dayin when you reach" |

## By hour
- Morning: chah, "jaldi uth gaya?", school and work. Afternoon: heat, lunch, "kamm kaar". Evening: "ghar nu chal", Mummy ji waiting. Night: "eni raat gaye?", go home, take care.

## Rules the game keeps
- One line, one voice: the words on screen are the words of the recording that plays. A literal line is shown only when there is no recording.
- Strangers stay quiet unless spoken to; named people greet by name.
- A repeated line waits at least 90 s (greetings excepted).
- Subtitles: Settings → Subtitles: words over the speaker (default), words plus a caption line at the bottom, or none. Lines from off-screen speakers (a loudspeaker, a passenger) always have the caption when captions are on.
- Radio and TV carry no words: music, a news sting and a murmur.
